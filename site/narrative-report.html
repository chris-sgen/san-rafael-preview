The report your attorney receives

What is in it, who writes it, and how to find out when it will land.

A narrative report is the written account of your examination: what happened to you, what was found when you were examined, what it was diagnosed as, what treatment was given, and where things stood when care ended. Your attorney works from that document. This page sets out what goes into it and who authors it.

An injury nobody wrote down

The usual path after a collision is to wait. Wait and see if it settles down, or see somebody for painkillers, or hold off on care until the claim is sorted out. The cost of waiting is not something we are going to describe in terms of your claim, because that is not ours to describe. We will describe it in terms of your file.

A gap between the collision and the first examination is a hole in the record. Nothing was written down during the period when the injury was at its worst. Whatever you remember later, nobody measured it at the time. The crash page covers why the first days matter.

"The patient reported feeling better" is not a finding. A record made of impressions cannot carry what a measured examination can. Range of motion in degrees, a named orthopedic test and what it returned, a neurological screen with a result: those read the same in six months as they did on the day. That is the whole argument of this page, and it is made once, here.

What the report lets you do

Your findings are recorded as measurements, not impressions.

You can point at a number taken on the day instead of describing how you felt. The record says the same thing later that it said at the start.

One named doctor authors it.

Dr. Michael Perry Joseph examines you and writes the document. There is a person to ask about it, not a clinic address.

You know the format before you commit to being treated here.

The contents are set out on this page in plain words, so you are choosing a practice on what it produces rather than on an adjective.

What is in the report

01

History and mechanism.

How the collision happened and what force went where: direction of impact, seat position, headrest, what you were doing at the moment of the crash.

02

Examination findings.

The orthopedic and neurological tests performed, and what each one returned. This is the measured part of the document.

03

Diagnosis and clinical reasoning.

What the findings add up to, written in the clinician's own words and in plain terms rather than in code.

04

Treatment given, and your response to it.

What was done, over what period, and what re-examination showed.

05

Status when care ended.

Where things stood at discharge.

This page is general information, not medical advice. An examination is needed to know whether we can help you.

What to ask any clinic you are considering

This is a short list because it is meant to be used, on this practice as much as on anyone else.

  1. Ask for the contents, not a promise. "We will document your injury and send a report to your attorney" is a sentence, not a specification. Ask what the document contains, what format it comes in, and when it is produced.
  2. Ask what imaging is for. Some clinics sell scanning as evidence for the claim. We do not: imaging is ordered when the examination indicates it, it is optional, shielding is provided, and the price is put in writing before it is taken.
  3. Ask who writes it. A document assembled from three people's notes reads like three people disagreeing. Here, the doctor who examines you is the doctor who writes it.

Questions people actually ask

Do I need an attorney to get one?

No. The record exists because you were examined. Whether you engage a lawyer is your decision and we do not arrange one for you.

How long does it take?

No published figure, for the reason given under what this page does not publish yet. Ask for the date when you request the report.

Does it cost extra?

There is no separate rate card on this site for the report or for anything else. Care here runs on a lien: you are billed nothing while your case is open, the practice is paid from the settlement, and if the case is not accepted, is lost, or settles for less than the bill, the balance is written off in full and you owe nothing. The cost page sets that out in full.

Will the doctor testify, or give an opinion for my case?

This practice makes no claim of that kind and holds no credential of that kind on record. What it produces is the clinical document described above. Anything beyond that document is a question for your attorney.

Is this legal advice?

No. This practice is not a law firm, offers no legal advice and no representation, and does not talk about what a case is worth.
