The driver who hit you has no insurance. You can still be treated.

Coverage on your own policy may be able to respond. It is called uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, and it exists for exactly this. This page explains how it works, and what to do about your injury while you are still finding out whether you have it.

What "uninsured" actually means for your care

There are three versions of this, and most people only know the first.

The other driver carried no policy at all.

Or they carried one and it will not respond. Either way there is no third party to send the bill to.

The other driver was underinsured.

They had a policy, but its limit is smaller than what the injury costs. This is the more common version, and it is the one people have usually not heard of. It catches out people who were told at the scene that the other driver "has insurance" and assumed that settled it.

Hit and run.

Many policies treat a driver who left the scene as an uninsured-motorist situation. That is a general characteristic of the coverage type, not a statement about your policy. Yours is written the way it is written.

Underneath all three sits a fact about this state. California is an at-fault state with no personal injury protection. There is no automatic no-fault pot of money for medical care after a crash here. Somebody has to be responsible, and if the person responsible has nothing, the coverage on your own policy is what is left. That is why this question matters more in California than it would somewhere else.

How uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage works

The coverage sits on your own auto policy, not on the other driver's. In California it is optional, so it is not automatic, and a lot of people carry it without realising.

Its purpose is to respond when the at-fault driver cannot cover the injury. That is what the coverage type is designed to do. It is not a prediction of what will be paid in your case, and nobody should offer you one.

We do not read your policy for you and we do not advise on coverage.

To find out whether you have it, look at the declarations page of your own policy, or ask your carrier or your attorney. We can tell you what the coverage type generally does. What yours does is between you and the people who wrote it.

It does not replace medical payments coverage, and it does not run on a fast clock. A claim under it can take as long as any other claim to resolve. That is exactly why the funding question and the treatment question are answered separately, and why the next section exists.

What pays for your care while that is being sorted out

Three routes, and one of them almost always applies.

Medical payments coverage on your own policy, where you have it.

It generally responds regardless of fault, which usually makes it the quickest route open to you after a collision.

A lien against the settlement, where we accept the case on one.

We treat you now and are paid later out of the settlement, under a written agreement. We decide case by case, and you get a yes or a no before treatment starts rather than after.

Paying for it yourself, with the expected cost given to you in writing before the examination.

We publish no rate card, because what a collision case costs depends on what the examination finds.

On the lien route, these are the terms, and they belong here rather than three clicks away. While your case is open you are billed nothing, and we are paid from the settlement. If we cannot accept the case, if the case is lost, or if it settles for less than the bill, the balance is written off in full and you owe nothing. All three end the same way. A lien is not a giveaway, though: the care is billed, and what changes is who the bill goes to and when.

What to do in the meantime

The coverage question and the treatment question run on different clocks, and this is the part worth getting right.

Coverage takes weeks. Adjusters open files, policies get read, letters go back and forth. The record of your injury, on the other hand, is made in days. An examination that finds and dates your findings is what everything later gets read against, and the interval between the crash and that first examination is called a treatment gap. A long one is routinely used to argue that the injury was not serious, whoever ends up paying.

So the order is: get examined and get the findings written down, then let the coverage question resolve behind it. You do not need to know who is paying before you can be seen.
