The first 72 hours after a collision

What to do, in order. And why the gap between the crash and your first examination matters more than most people are told.

What to do in the first days, in order

01

Red flags go to an emergency room, not to us.

Loss of consciousness, a blow to the head, confusion, numbness or weakness that is spreading, weakness in an arm or a leg, loss of bladder or bowel control, severe chest or abdominal pain, or a headache unlike any you have had before. This practice is not an emergency room and this step comes before anything else on this page. What a chiropractor does and does not do is set out on the doctor page.

02

Exchange details and record what happened at the scene.

Names, insurers, policy numbers, plate numbers, photographs of both vehicles and of the road, and the police report number if a report is taken. Write it down or photograph it while you are still there, because almost nobody remembers this list under stress.

03

Tell your own insurer, and ask whether your policy carries MedPay.

One phone call. What MedPay is and how it fits with the other funding rails is explained on how care is paid for.

04

Expect symptoms to arrive late.

Delayed onset is normal after a collision. Neck and upper back pain, headache, shoulder pain, and the numbness or tingling of an irritated nerve commonly begin twelve to seventy-two hours later, once the adrenaline has gone and the inflammation has developed. "I felt fine at the scene" is the most common sentence anyone says in this clinic, and it is not evidence that nothing happened to you. The injuries most often involved are whiplash, soft tissue injuries and herniated disc. Whiplash has its own page.

05

Get examined, and get the findings written down.

That is a separate decision from starting treatment, and you are allowed to make only the first one. What the appointment involves is set out step by step, including what it costs.

06

Keep everything.

Every bill, every mileage note, every day you could not work, every appointment card. It is tedious and it is the part people most regret skipping.

This page is general information, not medical advice. An examination is needed to know whether we can help you.

Why the gap between the crash and your first visit matters

An injury claim is assessed largely from the medical record. A period between the collision and the first documented examination is visible in that record as a gap. Adjusters and defence reviewers routinely read a gap as evidence that the injury was minor, or that it was not caused by the collision. Not because the person was not hurt, but because nothing was written down while they were.

That is unfair, and it is also real. Most people delay for exactly the right reasons. They walked away. They did not want to make a fuss. They assumed it would settle down in a week. The record does not capture the reason. It captures the gap.

"Documented" has a specific meaning here. It means a dated examination with objective findings, which are measurements and test results another clinician or an adjuster can read later: measured range of motion, orthopedic and neurological test results, and a written description of how the injury happened. That is what closes the gap. Whether you then start a course of care is a decision you make afterwards, with the findings in front of you.

The honest limit, which belongs in the same section as the argument: getting examined early does not guarantee anything about a claim, and nothing on this site predicts what a claim will do. A gap is one of several things a reviewer looks at. The reason this argument is worth making plainly is that the practices which make it loudest tend to attach a promise to it, and we do not.

The short version of this question also sits in the questions people ask, which is where the whole set lives.

What people do instead, and what this page will not promise you

There are three default paths after a collision, and each has a cost.

Do nothing and hope it settles down.

Sometimes it does. The cost when it does not is the gap described above, which by then cannot be closed retrospectively.

Painkillers only.

Medication manages pain. Our aim is the mechanical injury causing it. Both have a place, and for some injuries you need both, so this is not an argument against your prescription.

Wait until the insurance claim is resolved.

This is the one that goes wrong most quietly. Settle before the injury has resolved and you can be left paying for the rest of your care yourself, out of a settlement that was calculated without it.

What this page will and will not tell you about our calendar. New auto accident patients are seen the same day. That commitment covers new auto accident patients only. The practice's published opening hours are Tuesday to Friday, 9am to 6pm, and open doors on four weekdays are not a booking promise for anybody else.

What we stopped saying. The site this page replaces carried a set of claims about speed, about outcomes, and about what insurers pay. When the practice went through them, the instruction was to drop all of them, and they are gone rather than softened. The clock this page argues about is the one on your medical record, not the one on our appointment book.
