What a collision does to your neck

In a collision your neck is forced backward and then forward faster than the muscles and ligaments can control it. The load lands on soft tissue and on the small joints of the neck. That is the reason a serious whiplash injury commonly does not appear on an X-ray or an MRI: the structures that took the force are not the ones that show up well on an image. It is also the reason speed is a poor guide. A car park shunt can do it, and often does.

What people describe to us afterwards:

  • a neck that will not turn as far as it did
  • stiffness that is worse the second morning than the first
  • pain into the shoulder, arm or hand
  • dizziness
  • ringing in the ears
  • unusual tiredness
  • changes in vision
  • pins and needles

Not everyone has all of it, and having some of it does not tell you how bad the injury is. That is what an examination is for.

Why the first days matter

Two things are true about whiplash, and both matter more than they sound.

The first: symptoms very often start hours or days after the crash. Adrenaline at the scene masks them. So "I felt fine" is not evidence you were not hurt, and it is not something to be embarrassed about saying to the officer or the adjuster. It is the normal pattern.

The second: the interval between the collision and your first examination is an interval with nothing in your medical record. The record is the thing your claim rests on. A week with no entry in it is a week that has to be explained later, from memory, by you.

That is why sooner is better. It is an argument about your paperwork and your recall, not a statement about our schedule. The full version of it, including what to do in the first 72 hours, is here: what to do after a crash.

What the examination covers

Range of motion and orthopedic testing.

We measure how far your neck moves in each direction and which movements reproduce your pain, then test the structures that could be responsible. A measured figure written down on the day beats "it hurt a lot back then" six months later, both for your care and for your file.

Neurological screening.

Reflexes, sensation and strength in the arms and hands. Neck injuries refer down the arm, so a normal-looking neck with a weak grip is a finding, and so is a numb patch you had not mentioned because it did not hurt.

Imaging, only if it is indicated.

X-rays may be recommended after this examination, and they are optional. What they cost, and the shielding we use, are set out in one block on the first-visit page rather than buried here: your first visit

What care involves, and what it does not

Settle the inflamed tissue first.

Early on, an irritated neck does not want to be moved hard. The first phase is aimed at reducing that irritation so the neck tolerates being worked on at all.

Soft-tissue work and graded movement.

Hands-on work through the neck and shoulder musculature, plus movement that is increased in steps as the neck allows it, rather than all at once.

Spinal manipulation, once the neck is less inflamed.

A controlled movement applied to a joint by hand, to restore movement at a segment that is not moving well. We explain what we are doing before we do it, and we do not do it while the neck is too irritated for it.

Home instructions.

Ice, short-term support, and what to avoid in the first weeks. Most of what happens to a neck injury happens between visits.

Painkillers do a job, and that job is the pain. Ours is the mechanical injury underneath it. Both have a place, and after some collisions you need both. If the examination points somewhere else, a fracture, a head injury, or a nerve injury that needs imaging we do not perform, we say so and tell you who to see. We describe that referral process honestly: we make the call and send you on. We are not going to imply a formal network we cannot name.

What gets written down for your claim

Everything the examination finds is recorded. Not "the patient reported neck pain", but the measurements: degrees of movement, which tests were positive, what the neurological screen showed, and what changed between visits.

Beyond the file itself, this practice produces a narrative report, the written medical-legal summary an attorney uses. It sets out the mechanism of the collision, the examination findings, the course of care and the clinical reasoning connecting the injury to the crash. It is a separate piece of work from your treatment notes, and it is written by the doctor who examined you.

How long it takes, and what one contains in detail, are stated in one place so there is only one version to keep true: the narrative report

We will speak with your attorney about your care. We are not a law firm, and nothing here is legal guidance. Whether you need an attorney is not our call to make.

What it costs, and who pays

Before we examine you, you get a written Good Faith Estimate: what it covers, what would be billed separately, and who owes what. You do not have to ask for it.

After a collision, care is usually funded through one of five routes:

  • MedPay on your own auto policy, if you have it.
  • The at-fault driver's liability cover, paid at the end rather than as you go.
  • Your own uninsured or underinsured motorist cover, which is the route almost nobody mentions.
  • A lien against the personal-injury settlement.
  • Self-pay, if you would rather keep insurers out of it.

All five are resolved in one place: how care is paid for

Care here runs on a lien. You are billed nothing while your case is open, and we are paid out of the settlement. If the case is not accepted, is lost, or settles for less than the bill, the balance is written off in full and you owe nothing. That is the arrangement in plain words, and it is the reason this page can talk about examinations without talking about your credit card.

Questions people actually ask

It did not hurt at the scene. Is it too late?

No. Starting later is the normal pattern for whiplash, for the reason set out above. What matters now is that the injury gets examined and recorded rather than reconstructed from memory in six months.

Nothing showed on my X-ray. Does that mean I am fine?

Not on its own. Soft-tissue and joint injury frequently does not show on an X-ray, which is why the examination measures movement, tests the structures directly and screens the nerves. Those findings are what go in the file. If an X-ray is recommended for you, it is optional, and what it costs and the shielding we use are set out together here: the first visit page

What if I do not have an attorney?

We see people with and without one. Having an attorney is not a condition of being examined here. We are not a law firm, and we do not steer you into any particular one.

What if my insurance denies it?

We cannot tell you what a carrier will decide, and we will not pretend to. What we can tell you is what we do about it: care is on a lien, you are billed nothing while the case is open, and if the case is not accepted or is lost the balance is written off in full and you owe nothing.

How long does care take?

That depends on what the examination finds, and we will tell you what we expect once we have examined you. We do not put a recovery time on a page, because we would be making it up.
