Low back pain after a car accident

It often starts a day or two later, which is exactly why people decide it was nothing. Here is how we examine it, what it costs to find out, and what gets written down.

What a collision does to your low back

A crash loads the low back in ways ordinary life does not. Three of them are common enough to describe:

01

The lap belt holds your pelvis while the rest of you keeps moving.

The belt does its job, which is to keep you in the seat. The consequence is that the pelvis stops and the trunk does not, and the low back takes the difference.

02

The spine is loaded forward and then back in under a second.

There is no time to brace properly, and the tissue that would normally share the load does not get the chance.

03

Most people are turned or braced at the moment of impact.

Reaching for the wheel, looking at a mirror, half-turned to a passenger. A twisted low back loads one side far harder than the other, and that asymmetry is often what we find on examination.

What can be injured: the muscles and ligaments that hold the lumbar spine, which is the section of your back below the ribs; the discs, which are the cushions between the vertebrae; and the facet joints, the small paired joints at the back of the spine that guide how it moves.

Why it often starts days later

At the scene, adrenaline is doing a lot of work. Plenty of people drive home, sleep badly, and wake on the second or third morning barely able to get out of bed. The low back in particular tends to stiffen over the first one to three days rather than hurting at once.

Which creates a problem that has nothing to do with your back. Those days are days with nothing in your medical record. The record is what a claim rests on. When there is a gap in it, the gap is the part you end up explaining, months later, from memory.

So being examined early is worth something even if you are not sure the pain justifies it. That is an argument about documentation, not a claim about our schedule. The longer version, including what to do in the first 72 hours: what to do after a crash

What the examination covers

Movement and orthopedic testing.

How far you can bend and rotate, which directions reproduce the pain, and which tests load the disc, the joint or the muscle specifically. A measured restriction written down on the day is worth more than a description of how bad it was six months ago.

Neurological screening of the legs.

Reflexes, sensation and strength. A low-back injury can refer down the leg, and when it does, the pattern tells us which level is involved. If that is what is happening to you, this page goes further: sciatica

Imaging only if it is indicated.

X-rays may be recommended, and they are optional. The cost and the shielding we use are set out together in one block, on the page that owns them: your first visit

The findings are written down as findings, and we will speak with your attorney about your care.

What care involves, and what it does not

Settle it down first.

An acutely irritated low back is not ready to be worked on hard. The first phase is aimed at reducing that irritation.

Soft-tissue work and graded movement.

Hands-on work, then movement reintroduced in steps. Backs that are protected completely tend to stay stiff; backs pushed too early tend to flare. The line between those is the clinical judgement you are paying for.

Spinal manipulation to the lumbar spine and pelvis, once it is tolerated.

A controlled movement applied by hand to a joint that is not moving well. We tell you what we are about to do, and why, before we do it.

Home instructions.

How to sit, how to get in and out of a car without setting it off, and what to expect between visits.

Medication manages pain. Our aim is to address the mechanical injury causing it. Both have a place, and for some injuries you need both. If the examination points to a fracture, a nerve injury that needs imaging we do not perform, or anything outside musculoskeletal care, we say so and tell you who to see. We will describe how that referral works. We will not imply a network of specialists we cannot name.

What it costs, and who pays

Before we examine you, you get a written Good Faith Estimate: what it covers, what would be billed separately, and who owes what.

Care after a collision is usually funded through one of five routes, and all five are resolved in one place rather than scattered: MedPay on your own policy; the at-fault driver's liability cover; your own uninsured or underinsured motorist cover; a lien against the personal-injury settlement; or self-pay. The third of those is the one nobody in this market seems to mention, and it is the one that saves people whose crash was caused by a driver with no cover. How care is paid for

Care here runs on a lien. You are billed nothing while the case is open, and we are paid from the settlement. If the case is not accepted, is lost, or settles for less than the bill, the balance is written off in full and you owe nothing.

Questions people actually ask

It started three days after the crash. Is that normal?

Yes, commonly. The low back tends to stiffen over the following days rather than hurting at once. Late onset is not evidence that nothing happened, and it is not something you need to apologise for.

Do I need an attorney to be seen here?

No. We see people with and without one, and we are not a law firm. If you do have an attorney, we will speak with them about your care.

What if the other driver had no insurance?

There is a route for that: your own uninsured or underinsured motorist cover. It has its own page, because it is the one people are least often told about. When the other driver is uninsured

Will you tell me what it costs before you examine me?

Yes, in writing, before anything starts. That is what the Good Faith Estimate is for, and you do not have to ask for it. The written estimate
