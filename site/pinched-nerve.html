Numbness or tingling since the crash

This page is general information, not medical advice. An examination is needed to know whether we can help you.

Pins and needles, a patch that feels dead, a shooting pain down one arm or one leg. "Pinched nerve" is the phrase people search for; a compressed nerve is what it usually means. This is the San Rafael practice for auto-accident injuries. Before we examine you, the price of the evaluation goes in writing.

01

The symptom people wait out

Numbness and tingling get dismissed more than any other symptom after a collision, and the reason is simple: they do not hurt the way a broken thing hurts. So people wait. They do nothing and hope it settles, or they take what urgent care handed them, or they decide to deal with it once the insurance claim is finished. All three are reasonable-sounding, and none of them puts anything in your medical record.

Then there is the trap specific to this symptom. Nerve symptoms come and go. The numbness that appears at two in the morning, or only after twenty minutes of driving, is the easiest thing in the world to describe as "it comes and goes" and the hardest thing to reconstruct four months later.

An adjuster can argue away a symptom that was never written down. It is much harder to argue away a distribution that was mapped and dated while it was recent.

And that is the whole timing argument on this page. The interval between the crash and the first documented examination is an interval with nothing in the record. It is about the record, not about our schedule.

02

Three things you can do after reading this page

Find out where the compression actually is.

Pain and numbness are frequently felt a long way from the structure causing them. A nerve root irritated in your neck can present as forearm and hand symptoms; one irritated in your low back can present in the calf and foot. What that lets you do: stop chasing the place that hurts and get the place that is injured examined.

Know the cost before you commit.

The price of the evaluation goes in writing before the examination. How it is funded, and what happens if the claim does not work out, is answered in full on the money page.

Get an intermittent symptom onto the record while it is recent.

Distribution, what provokes it, whether anything measurable is weak. Written down as objective findings rather than recalled as an impression.

What the examination actually consists of

Card 1. The symptom map.

Before you come in, it is worth answering these. Most people have never been asked, and the answers are half the diagnosis:

  • Where does it start, and where does it travel to?
  • Is it constant, or does it come and go?
  • What brings it on: a position, a time of day, driving, sleeping?
  • Is anything actually weak, as opposed to numb?
  • What were you doing at the moment of the impact, and which way were you facing?

03

Card 2. The neurological examination.

Sensation, reflexes and strength testing through the affected distribution, and the spinal segments that correspond to it. That combination is what separates a nerve that is genuinely compressed from a muscle that is irritable.

Care is aimed at relieving pain and restoring normal movement.

Card 3. When imaging is warranted, and who does it.

Not everybody needs a scan, and ordering one before an examination usually just delays the answer. If what we find warrants an image, we tell you why and we send you for it, and somebody else performs it. We are not going to name facilities or imply a formal network we cannot stand behind.

04

Card 4. Where we stop.

We are not a medical doctor's office: no surgery, no prescriptions. We are not a law firm, and this is not an emergency room.

Some nerve symptoms are urgent in a way we are not the answer to: weakness that is getting worse by the day, a foot or a hand that will not hold, loss of bowel or bladder control, numbness through the saddle area. Those mean emergency care, not an appointment here.

Around the care itself we can truthfully offer orthopaedic and neurological examination, chiropractic adjustment, which is a controlled movement applied by hand to a joint that is not moving well, insurance verification, medical-lien billing and coordination with your attorney.

What a first appointment involves is set out here: your first visit.

Questions a nerve-symptom page actually receives

The tingling comes and goes. Is it worth being examined?

Yes, and the intermittency is the reason rather than an argument against it. What gets recorded is the distribution, what provokes it and anything measurable, dated to when it was happening. A symptom nobody wrote down is a symptom you have to argue for from memory.

Do I need an MRI first?

No. The order of operations runs the other way: examine first, then image if the examination warrants it, performed by whoever does the imaging. Arriving with a scan and no examination is a common and expensive way to answer the wrong question.

Who pays for this?

Case by case, through one of three channels: auto or health insurance, a medical lien against the personal-injury settlement, or cash. This practice publishes no rate card, and the routes are set out properly in one place. How care is paid for.

What if the claim fails, or settles for less than the bill?

You owe nothing. Care runs on a lien: you are billed nothing while the case is open, we are paid from the settlement, and if the case is not accepted, is lost, or settles for less than the bill, the balance is written off in full.

Do I need an attorney before you will see me?

No. We are not a law firm and having representation is not a condition of being examined here. If you do have an attorney, we will speak with them about your care.
