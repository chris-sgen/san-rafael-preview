Your price, in writing, before we examine you

Before we examine you, you can have a written estimate of what your care is expected to cost. This page explains what is on that document, what it commits us to, and what it does not.

What a Good Faith Estimate is

A Good Faith Estimate is a written statement of the costs a course of care is expected to involve, given to you before the care happens.

The obligation comes from the federal No Surprises Act, which requires health care providers to put expected charges in writing for patients who are not having their care billed through health insurance.

That description covers a lot of people injured in a collision. If your care is going to be paid out of a claim rather than run through a health plan, you are the person the rule was written for, and the estimate is yours to ask for.

It is an estimate of expected cost. It is not a bill, it is not a contract, and it is not a cap on what care can ever cost. It is a considered, written answer to the question "what am I looking at here", produced before you have to decide anything.

Our own position on it is short. We offer it to anyone who asks, and we offer it before the examination rather than after. This practice has published the statutory notice for years. Making it a promise you can actually use is the point of this page.

Get your estimate in writing

What is on yours, and when you get it

The estimate is written for your case, so the line items follow what your case is likely to need. It names each of these, with the cost of each one stated on the document:

  1. 01

    The initial consultation, and whether it is charged to your case.

  2. 02

    The examination itself, orthopedic and neurological.

  3. 03

    A diagnostic X-ray, if one is recommended.

    Imaging is recommended when the examination points to something an X-ray would settle. It is optional and you may decline it. What it costs is stated in writing before it is taken. Shielding is provided.

    The longer explanation of how imaging is handled here is on your first visit.

  4. 04

    A course of treatment, described as a range of visits rather than a single number.

    The range depends on what the examination finds, which is the honest way to write it.

  5. 05

    Anything billed separately: copies of your records or films, and a report prepared for a claim where one is requested.

When you get it.

Before the examination, on request, and in writing. Not read aloud at the front desk, and not quoted over the phone as the only record of what you were told.

What is not on it, deliberately.

There is no rate card on this website and there never will be one. The estimate is written for your case, and a menu price on a web page would be a guess about somebody else. Two of the things a guess does is go stale and get contradicted by the page next to it. A number written for you does neither.

What it does and does not commit us to

This is the section that decides whether the rest of the page is worth anything.

It is an estimate, not a guarantee of the final cost.

If the examination finds something that changes the plan, we tell you what changed and what it costs before it happens, not on the invoice afterwards.

It does not commit you to treatment.

Reading the estimate and deciding not to go ahead is a normal outcome and we plan for it. The estimate exists so the decision is yours to make with the facts in front of you.

It does not predict what an insurer will pay.

Coverage depends on the policy and the circumstances of the crash. We verify benefits and tell you what applies to you. We do not promise what any carrier will do, and no one who does should be believed.

And it is not how you get billed.

Where care is provided on a lien, you are billed nothing while your case is open and we are paid from the settlement. If we cannot accept the case, if the case is lost, or if it settles for less than the bill, the balance is written off in full and you owe nothing. The estimate tells you what the care costs. It does not put that cost on your doorstep.

The two disclaimers this practice carries, in full: Results are not guaranteed and may vary from person to person, and testimonials apply only to the individuals depicted.

Why this is the whole argument

You should not have to decide whether to be examined without knowing what it costs. And you should not have to take that number on trust from a phone call you cannot produce later. A written estimate handed over before the examination is the smallest honest version of both, and it is the reason this page sits in the middle of the site instead of at the bottom of it.

It also only answers half the question. The estimate says what the care costs. How care is paid for says who pays for it, across all five funding routes. Both halves get answered before anything starts, which is the entire offer.
